Focal Lesions & HCC-Specific Findings

02Importance of the liver background in the setting of a lesion

A single well-defined and evenly bright lesion may be confidently called a hemangioma on ultrasound alone only in a patient with no symptoms, no history of cancer and no risk factors for liver tumors. However, in a patient with cirrhosis or hepatitis B, the same appearance is not as reassuring. In this setting, HCC can also be bright, particularly in a cirrhotic liver. This is why it is important to note the appearance of the liver overall on ultrasound, not just any focal lesions.

03HCC on ultrasound

HCC has no single appearance on ultrasound. In published studies of conventional ultrasound surveillance, ultrasound found about 84% of HCC at any stage, but only about 47% of early-stage HCC, rising to about 63% when AFP is added (meta-analysis cited in the LI-RADS guidance)[128]. That is why technique appears to possibly play a large role and why repeat scans matter so much.

04Benign lesions

Simple cyst

A simple cyst usually has no internal echoes, is of a round or oval shape, with a smooth thin wall and brighter tissue behind it (posterior enhancement). Thin dark lines from its edges (called edge shadowing) often support it but are not required. Color Doppler showing no flow inside is a useful check as well. Anything more, such as septations, a nodule on the wall, a solid part, or echoes that persist when the cyst is centered in the beam indicates that it is not a simple cyst. In HCC surveillance, an observation that is not definitely benign follows US LI-RADS size criteria: if <10 mm → US-2 and if ≥10 mm → US-3, unless already characterized as benign.

Hemangioma

The most common benign liver tumor. Typically small, evenly hyperechoic, well-defined, often with posterior enhancement. In a fatty liver it can look darker than the liver around it. In a patient under surveillance, only a hemangioma already confirmed on contrast imaging counts as definitely benign.

Hydatid cyst

Hydatid cysts are caused by Echinococcus granulosus. Characteristic ultrasound findings include daughter cysts within a parent cyst or a detached membrane floating inside the cyst, also known as the “water-lily” sign. Cysts are staged using the WHO-IWGE ultrasound classification (CE1–CE5), which classifies stage, size, location, and complications to guide management. Early cysts may resemble simple cysts, so consider hydatid disease when exposure history or residence in an endemic area raises suspicion.

Liver abscess

Liver abscesses have a variable ultrasound appearance. Amebic abscesses are often solitary, round or oval, and located peripherally in the right hepatic lobe, with low-level internal echoes and no clearly defined echogenic wall. Pyogenic abscesses may appear as complex fluid collections containing debris or septations. Sometimes gas is present which produces bright echoes with reverberation or dirty shadowing. Early abscesses may appear solid.

Ultrasound alone cannot reliably distinguish amebic from pyogenic abscesses, and necrotic tumors can mimic either type. Diagnosis therefore combines imaging with clinical findings and appropriate laboratory testing, including bacterial cultures and amebic serology or molecular testing when available.

Amebic abscesses are particularly important in endemic tropical regions, and liver abscess is the most common extraintestinal manifestation of amebiasis.

Calcified granuloma and focal fatty sparing

A small bright focus with clean posterior acoustic shadowing and no associated soft-tissue mass is consistent with a benign calcification, often a healed granuloma. Focal fatty sparing is also benign when it has a characteristic location and geographic or wedge-shaped appearance, with vessels passing through normally and no mass effect. These are benign observations and require no further investigation. Patients at risk for HCC should continue routine surveillance, provided liver visualization is adequate and there are no other concerning findings.

Practice: name the lesion

RevealLesion A
Hyperechoic, oval, sharply demarcated: the classic hemangioma appearance. In a patient under surveillance it still counts as not definitely benign unless a hemangioma was already confirmed on contrast imaging.
RevealLesion B
Hypoechoic solid lesions. You cannot definitively name these on B-mode. Suspected malignancy until proven otherwise, with a differential of metastasis (often with a hypoechoic halo) versus an atypical hemangioma, which can look hypoechoic in a fatty liver.
RevealLesion C
Anechoic with a smooth margin and posterior enhancement, a simple cyst.

05LI-RADS US Surveillance v2024

Every surveillance ultrasound gets two labels: a category for the whole exam, based on its most worrying finding, and a visualization score for how well you could see the liver.

Record the segment and two measurements of every observation, so the next scan can compare.

Check yourselfShould this patient be referred for CT? A cirrhotic patient's nodule measured 8 mm six months ago. Today it measures 13 mm.

Yes, because it has crossed the 10 mm threshold, which under US LI-RADS v2024 makes it US-3 and warrants referral for diagnostic multiphase imaging. This is different from a nodule that grows while remaining under 10 mm: a 5 mm to 9 mm observation stays US-2, and the recommended path is two short-interval ultrasound examinations at 3 to 6 months rather than immediate CT or MRI referral. Size crossing the threshold, not growth alone, is what triggers escalation here.

06When you are not sure

  • Confirm it in a second plane and from a second window. A finding that will not reproduce may be an artifact.
  • Store images and a cine clip through it.
  • Escalate for over-read.

Week 2 · Session 4

Focal Lesions & HCC-Specific Findings

By the end of this session you should be able to

  • Describe the sonographic appearance of potential HCC lesions and why such lesions are often subtle
  • Recognize other possible benign lesions
  • Explain why background liver changes the meaning of an identical appearance

01Pre-test probability

A uniformly hyperechoic 2 cm lesion with posterior enhancement in a healthy 30-year-old is most likely a hemangioma. The identical appearance in a 55-year-old with HBV cirrhosis is not as reassuring, because HCC can be hyperechoic and the background liver has changed the probability.

Assumptions that fail in the setting of cirrhosis "Hyperechoic means hemangioma" may be a reasonable assumption in a normal liver but can be an unsafe assumption in a cirrhotic liver.

02HCC on Ultrasound

There is no single appearance, which is why ultrasound sensitivity for early HCC is limited. However, you can detect lesions that may be considered concerning for possible malignancy based on appearance, allowing for further referral and appropriate follow-up imaging and/or testing, which is the goal of this program.

03Benign lesions

01Simple cyst

The full criteria set, worth running through explicitly rather than pattern-matching on brightness alone:

  • Anechoic, no internal echoes
  • Round or oval shape
  • Smooth, imperceptibly thin wall
  • Posterior acoustic enhancement, the beam is barely attenuated by fluid, so the far wall and tissue beyond it look brighter than the surroundings
  • Edge shadowing at the lateral margins, where the beam refracts around the curved interface
  • No Doppler signal within the lesion, confirm this rather than assume it from the B-mode appearance

A cyst with thin septations is not strictly a simple cyst, though thin, smooth septations without nodularity are more reassuring than mural nodularity or a solid component, which are what most clearly make a cyst complex and worth referring. Remember slice-thickness artifact from Session 1: apparent internal debris that vanishes when the cyst is centered in the beam is not real.

02Hemangioma, and the limit of a benign appearance

Round, well-circumscribed, uniformly hyperechoic, often with posterior enhancement. The most common benign solid liver lesion.

03Hydatid cyst

A cystic lesion containing daughter cysts, or a detached undulating membrane (the "water-lily" sign).

04Hepatic abscess

Thick-walled, complex, hypoechoic collection with internal debris, in a patient who is usually febrile and unwell. Pyogenic or amoebic.

05Calcified granuloma

A small brightly echogenic focus with dense posterior shadowing and no mass effect. Incidental, requires no follow-up, and should not generate a recall.

RevealLesion A
Hyperechoic, oval, sharply demarcated, the classic hemangioma appearance.
RevealLesion B
Hypoechoic and less well defined, the appearance that raises concern for a malignant lesion such as HCC in an at-risk liver.
RevealLesion C
Anechoic with a smooth margin and posterior enhancement, a simple cyst.

Work through all three in order and notice that echogenicity alone does not decide anything: A and B differ mainly in brightness, but what actually changes management is the background liver and the size, not the echo pattern by itself.

04Measurement

Everything downstream depends on getting the number right and recording where you got it.

The flowchart above is the simplified shape. The full US LI-RADS v2024 categories, precisely:

  • Observation under 10 mm and not definitely benign: repeat ultrasound in 3–6 months.
  • Observation 10 mm or greater and not definitely benign: refer for diagnostic multiphasic contrast-enhanced CT or MRI.
  • Also refer: new portal or hepatic vein thrombus, or focal parenchymal distortion.
  • Definitely benign observation (a simple cyst, a previously characterized hemangioma): continue routine surveillance regardless of size.

Under v2024, a subthreshold (US-2) observation may be re-scanned up to twice at 3–6-month intervals. If it disappears, or stays under 10 mm on two consecutive exams, the patient returns to routine six-month surveillance. Interval growth to 10 mm or more triggers referral regardless of the original size. (ACR LI-RADS US Surveillance, v2024.)

Record approximate location or Couinaud segment, as well as measurements, so the lesion can be evaluated for interval change at the next visit.

Check yourselfShould this patient be referred for CT? A cirrhotic patient's nodule measured 8 mm six months ago. Today it measures 13 mm.

Yes, because it has crossed the 10 mm threshold, which under US LI-RADS v2024 makes it US-3 and warrants referral for diagnostic multiphase imaging. This is different from a nodule that grows while remaining under 10 mm: a 5 mm to 9 mm observation stays US-2, and the recommended path is two short-interval ultrasound examinations at 3 to 6 months rather than immediate CT or MRI referral. Size crossing the threshold, not growth alone, is what triggers escalation here.

05When you are not sure

  • Confirm in a second plane and from a second window. A finding that will not reproduce is a candidate artifact.
  • Store the images.
  • Escalate for over-read.

Week 2 · Session 4a

Beyond the Liver: Pancreas, Gallbladder, Spleen and Nodes

By the end of this session you should be able to

  • Find the pancreas using the splenic vein, and recognize a dilated duct
  • Explain the double duct sign and why it needs prompt referral
  • Tell gallstones from polyps, and know which polyps need surgery
  • Measure the spleen in its true long axis
  • Recognize the features that make a lymph node suspicious

01Pancreas

Finding it

On the transverse epigastric view, find the splenic vein. The body and tail of the pancreas lie directly anterior to the splenic vein. The pancreas head will lie to the patient’s right near the confluence, the body across the midline, the tail toward the splenic hilum. The young pancreas is about as bright as normal liver or slightly darker. Echogenicity often increases with age and fatty infiltration.

HeadAbout 2 cm

TailUnder 3 cm; up to 3.5 cm at the splenic hilum
Main pancreatic ductIn the body, about 2 mm is a practical reference, with some widening with age. The head and neck can measure more. For this reason, record where and how you measured, and the patient’s age.[99][100]

Five planes

Scanning viewTechnique and landmarks
1. Head-transverseIdentify the splenic vein–SMV confluence behind the pancreatic neck, then sweep toward the patient’s right to examine the head and uncinate process.
2. Head-longitudinal/obliqueRotate the probe and sweep through the head. Look for the distal common bile duct along its posterior aspect or within the pancreatic tissue.
3. Body-transverseExamine the body across the midline, anterior to the splenic vein.
4. Tail-anterior approachFollow the pancreatic body and splenic vein toward the patient’s left and the splenic hilum, adjusting probe angle as needed. A counterclockwise turn of the probe can help you follow the splenic vein and see the tail of the pancreas.
5. Tail-transsplenic approachScan from the left lateral intercostal region to assess the distal tail.

The double duct sign

Dilation of both the common bile duct and main pancreatic duct raises concern for obstruction from a pancreatic head or periampullary tumor. Benign causes include choledocholithiasis and chronic pancreatitis, therefore the double duct sign itself is not diagnostic of malignancy.

An abrupt cutoff of a dilated pancreatic duct is another suspicious finding, even when no mass is visible, and warrants expedited evaluation.

Document suspicious findings and escalate as needed

Measure and document both ducts and arrange prompt further evaluation and dedicated imaging if warranted, typically pancreatic-protocol CT or MRI/MRCP.

The bright pancreas

Increased pancreatic echogenicity can occur with aging and fatty infiltration and may be associated with obesity and metabolic risk factors. Brightness alone does not diagnose chronic pancreatitis. Findings must be interpreted alongside clinical history and other imaging findings. Fecal elastase testing is appropriate when exocrine pancreatic insufficiency is suspected. Marked echogenicity may also obscure the pancreatic duct.

Chronic pancreatitis

Ultrasound findings may include heterogeneous pancreatic tissue, shadowing calcifications, an irregular contour, an irregular or dilated main pancreatic duct, pseudocysts, and pancreatic atrophy in advanced disease. Calcifications are characteristic of established chronic pancreatitis, but early disease may have a normal ultrasound appearance. Neither normal ultrasound findings nor absence of tenderness excludes chronic pancreatitis. Any focal pancreatic mass requires further evaluation because inflammatory masses and pancreatic cancer can appear similar.

Acute pancreatitis

Acute pancreatitis is diagnosed when at least two of three criteria are present: characteristic abdominal pain, serum lipase or amylase at least three times the upper limit of normal, and characteristic imaging findings. The pancreas may appear normal on ultrasound or be obscured by bowel gas. When visible, findings may include enlargement, reduced echogenicity, indistinct margins, and surrounding fluid. Ultrasound cannot reliably identify or exclude pancreatic necrosis. CT is not routinely required at presentation when the diagnosis is clear. CT or MRI is appropriate when the diagnosis is uncertain, if the patient fails to improve within 48–72 hours after admission and initial treatment, or if complications are suspected.

Pseudocyst

Fluid collections in the first 4 weeks are acute collections. After 4 weeks, a collection with a wall and no dead tissue inside is considered a pseudocyst. When there is dead tissue and debris, it is walled-off necrosis. A pseudocyst is sharply outlined, anechoic, round or oval, with brighter tissue behind it.

02Gallbladder

  • Image it in two planes and two positions about 90° apart. A single plane often hides or mimics a stone, and turning the patient shows whether a stone moves. Look carefully at the neck.
  • Normal wall: 3 mm or less, measured on the anterior wall of a fasting gallbladder.

A gallbladder you cannot find

Four explanations: it was removed (ask); it is contracted after a meal (rescan fasting before calling anything); it is full of stones; or, rarely, congenital absence.

Thick wall

A thick wall alone is not acute cholecystitis. You can see wall thickening commonly with ascites, heart failure and hepatitis, all of which may be frequent in the patients you screen. A positive sonographic Murphy sign (tenderness when the probe presses on the gallbladder) supports acute cholecystitis, but its absence does not exclude it. Interpret other findings as well, such as stones, distension, the wall, fluid around the gallbladder (pericholecystic fluid) and the clinical picture together.

Risk factors: age over 60, primary sclerosing cholangitis, Asian ethnicity, and a sessile (flat-based) polyp, including focal wall thickening over 4 mm. If a polyp grows to 10 mm during follow-up, cholecystectomy is advised. Growth of 2 mm or more within the follow-up period should prompt reassessment. If there is no growth after two years, follow-up can stop.

Source:[129] The age risk factor was raised from 50 to 60. Other guidelines use polyp shape and differ in detail; follow your local pathway.

03Spleen

Measure the length between the ribs from the left side, rotating until you see the longest distance between the two poles.

A large spleen may not fit on the screen. Some machines have a panorama mode; if yours does not, record that the spleen extended beyond the field of view.

04Lymph nodes

You will pass several lymph nodes along the porta hepatis (along the portal vein), around the celiac trunk and superior mesenteric artery, the splenic hilum, and alongside the aorta and iliac arteries as you scan. Enlarged nodes next to a liver or pancreatic mass are useful information for the referral.

Beyond the Liver: Pancreas, Gallbladder, Spleen and Nodes

By the end of this session you should be able to

  • Locate the pancreas using the splenic vein, and recognize ductal dilatation
  • Explain what the double duct sign means and why it is urgent

01Pancreas

The pancreas is difficult, and nobody expects a screening operator to exclude pancreatic disease. What is expected is that you can find it, and that an obviously dilated duct or an obvious head mass does not go unrecognized.

Finding it

Find the splenic vein on the transverse epigastric view and the pancreas is draped over it along its entire length, head to the patient's right near the confluence, body across the midline, tail running toward the splenic hilum. The vein is the landmark; the gland follows.

Normal pancreas is homogeneous and slightly more echogenic than normal liver, becoming brighter and more fatty-replaced with age. It tapers head to tail.

MeasurementNormal adult upper limit
Head≤3.1 cm
Body≤2.0 cm AP, measured at the SMA
Tail≤3.0 cm, maximal orthogonal diameter

Five planes

  1. Head, transverse, at the confluence, to the patient's right
  2. Head, longitudinal, rotate; this is also where the distal bile duct appears in cross-section
  3. Body, transverse, across the midline, anterior to the splenic vein
  4. Tail, from the body view, rotate the probe anticlockwise and follow the splenic vein as it climbs toward the left. The vein does not run flat across the abdomen; it rises and wraps posteriorly toward the splenic hilum, and the tail wraps with it.
  5. Tail again, through the spleen, left intercostal, using the spleen as a window. This is your second look at the tail.
Two looks at the tail, deliberately The tail is the most common unimaged part of the pancreas, and pancreatic tail tumors are correspondingly the ones found late. You get one attempt anteriorly by following the vein, and a second from the splenic window when you scan the spleen anyway. Treat the splenic view as part of the pancreatic exam, not just the splenic one.

The double duct sign Dilatation of both the common bile duct and the main pancreatic duct warrants prompt evaluation for an obstructing lesion at the pancreatic head or ampulla. Malignancy is the most concerning cause, but benign causes, including stones, chronic pancreatitis, and strictures, can also produce this pattern.

You may see this in a patient referred for liver screening, particularly one presenting with jaundice. It is not a finding to schedule a repeat scan for. Document both duct diameters, and refer for cross-sectional imaging.

The echo-rich pancreas, often age-related, but context matters

Pancreatic echogenicity commonly increases with age, reflecting increasing fat deposition, fibrosis and parenchymal atrophy. In one ultrasound study, hyperechogenicity was present in 35% of subjects under 40 and 64% of those over 80, although these estimates came from small age subgroups. Increased echogenicity alone is nonspecific and does not establish pancreatic disease.

When it stops being reassuring The finding is sometimes labeled “lipomatosis,” and its relevance changes with context rather than appearance alone. Worth asking, not just noting the brightness: is there a metabolic syndrome? Any relevant clinical findings? Uniformly increased echogenicity in an asymptomatic older patient, without ductal dilatation, focal abnormality or other suspicious features, is often age-related and does not by itself require additional pancreatic imaging. Assess metabolic risk factors, and test for exocrine insufficiency when clinically suspected.

Two pathologies worth recognizing

01Chronic pancreatitis, and why it matters for cancer

Heterogeneous, patchily echogenic gland; coarse calcifications that shadow; an irregular, lumpy contour; often a dilated duct; and eventually a shrunken organ. Pseudocysts may be present. Characteristically not tender under the probe.

Why it is in this course. Two reasons, both about cancer. Chronic pancreatitis is a risk factor for pancreatic adenocarcinoma, and it is also the main differential for it, a mass-forming chronic pancreatitis and a tumor can look very similar on B-mode.

One discriminator worth knowing: duct dilatation in chronic pancreatitis tends to be irregular and beaded, a “string of pearls”. Smooth, uniform dilatation of the duct, especially with a dilated bile duct alongside, points toward an obstructing tumor instead. That distinction does not settle anything on its own, but it should change how urgently you escalate.

02Acute pancreatitis

In the first hours the gland may look entirely normal, ultrasound does not exclude it. When changes appear: a swollen, hypoechoic, edematous gland with blurred margins, peripancreatic fluid, and heterogeneous debris in more severe disease. Ultrasound may suggest edema or peripancreatic fluid, but it cannot reliably diagnose or quantify necrosis; contrast-enhanced CT or MRI is used when necrosis assessment is needed. Overlying ileus and bowel gas frequently defeat the exam altogether.

What ultrasound is actually for here. Diagnosis is clinical, supported by lipase. Ultrasound's jobs are different: look for a biliary cause (gallstones, a dilated duct), check the differential (an aneurysm can mimic this presentation), and look for complications.

A patient this unwell is not a screening patient. If you meet this picture during a screening clinic, the finding is an acute clinical referral, not a report.

03Pseudocyst

A pseudocyst is a late complication of edematous pancreatitis: a defined, encapsulated fluid collection without relevant necrotic components. It takes time to form, so it is not what you see on day one, it is what you see afterward, often incidentally, sometimes in a patient who never had a clearly documented acute episode.

The word "without relevant necrotic components" matters. A collection with debris, septations, or a thick irregular wall is not a simple pseudocyst by these criteria, it needs the same escalation as any other complex cystic finding, not a reassuring label borrowed from a different appearance.

What limits you

Overlying bowel gas defeats pancreatic imaging more often than any other abdominal organ, and the tail is the hardest part to see. Graded compression helps. If the pancreas was not adequately seen, document that.

02Gallbladder and gallstones

Gallbladder cancer is itself a hepatobiliary malignancy, uncommon, but with a poor prognosis when found late, and it can present as a polyp or a focally thickened wall long before it presents as a mass.

Scanning the gallbladder

  1. Transverse, via the interlobar fissure. Find the ligamentum teres, then the echogenic band of the interlobar fissure running toward the gallbladder fossa. The gallbladder sits at the end of that line.
  2. Subcostal on deep inspiration, supine or left lateral, the gallbladder slides down into view.
  3. Liver and kidney in longitudinal section, then medial to the kidney, subhepatic.
Two planes, two positions Image the gallbladder in two planes and in two patient positions roughly 90 degrees apart. A single plane very often contains an artifact that mimics or hides a stone, and repositioning is also how you test stone mobility. Pay deliberate attention to the neck and infundibulum, a stone impacted there is both the easiest to miss and the most likely to matter.

A gallbladder you cannot see at all

Four explanations, from most to least common:

  • Post-prandial contraction, the most common reason for a non-visualized gallbladder. A contracted gallbladder cannot exclude stones, so a fasting rescan is required before reporting anything.
  • Stone-filled, a densely shadowing arc where the gallbladder should be. Easy to mistake for bowel gas; the shadow is cleaner and the arc more defined.
  • Prior cholecystectomy, ask. This also raises the normal CBD limit to about 10 mm.
  • Agenesis, rare.

Wall thickness

Normal is under about 3 mm in a distended, fasting gallbladder. Above that, the differential is wide and mostly not cholecystitis:

Thickened wall, with...Suggests
Marked focal tenderness under the probeAcute cholecystitis
Ascites, cirrhosis, low albuminSecondary edema, not cholecystitis
Hepatitis, heart failure, systemic illnessSecondary edema
Focal, irregular, or asymmetric thickeningConcerning for malignancy, refer
Do not call cholecystitis on the image alone Wall thickening without focal tenderness on exam, and without a history consistent with acute biliary disease, is not acute cholecystitis. In a cirrhotic screening population with ascites and hypoalbuminemia, thickened walls are common and almost always secondary.

Why polyps matter here Most gallbladder polyps are small cholesterol polyps and are harmless. Size is what changes the conversation, and there are working thresholds worth knowing rather than deciding case by case:
  • Polyp 1 cm or larger: laparoscopic cholecystectomy, even if asymptomatic.
  • Polyp under 1 cm with biliary symptoms: cholecystectomy if the symptoms are plausibly gallbladder-related and no better cause is identified.
  • Polyp 6–9 mm plus a high-risk feature (age over 50, single sessile morphology, growth on follow-up): consider cholecystectomy.
Document the number, the largest size, and whether it is sessile or pedunculated, and route it to your referral pathway if needed.

03Spleen

You already measure the spleen as part of the liver protocol, because splenomegaly is an indirect sign of portal hypertension.

Common measurement error Measuring the spleen from an anterior approach systematically overestimates its size, because you are measuring an oblique through it rather than the true long axis.

Use a left lateral intercostal approach, rotated to the maximal longitudinal section between the poles, on quiet breathing or a gentle half-breath. Session 3 has the reference bands and a free calculator.

Beyond length

Length alone is the usual screening measurement and is enough for most purposes. Where a spleen is borderline, or where you want to track change over time, the additional dimensions are worth taking: depth measured perpendicular to the long axis at the hilum, and thickness as the third dimension. An approximate volume follows from length × depth × thickness × 0.5.

A genuinely enlarged spleen may exceed the probe's field of view entirely. Document that the spleen exceeded the field rather than only reporting a measurement you know is truncated.

04Lymph nodes

Nodal assessment matters here because it changes staging for HCC, cholangiocarcinoma and pancreatic cancer alike. Ultrasound will not stage based on nodal involvement, but a bulky nodal mass at the porta hepatis alongside a liver lesion, for example, is useful information for referral.

Where to look

You are already at the two stations that matter most for HPB disease: the porta hepatis and the celiac axis, both of which you pass through on the protocol. Also worth a glance: para-aortic and retroperitoneal nodes as you sweep the aorta.

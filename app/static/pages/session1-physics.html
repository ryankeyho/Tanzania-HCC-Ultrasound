Week 1 · Session 1

Physics & the Vscan Air CL

By the end of this session you should be able to

  • Explain how a B-mode image is formed, and what the brightness of a pixel actually represents
  • Choose probe and frequency for a given depth, and say why
  • Set depth and gain deliberately before scanning
  • Recognize the five artifacts you will meet repeatedly in the liver, and use two of them diagnostically

01How the image is made

The probe does two jobs, alternating thousands of times per second. Piezoelectric elements in its face deform when voltage is applied, producing a brief pulse of high-frequency sound. When that pulse meets a boundary between two tissues, part of it reflects and returns. The same elements convert the returning pressure wave back into voltage.

The machine essentially interprets two things about each returning echo: how long it took to come back, and how strong it was. From those two numbers it builds the image you see on the screen.

Echoes are produced at acoustic impedance mismatches, boundaries where tissue density and stiffness change. A large mismatch reflects almost everything (soft tissue to gas, soft tissue to bone); a small mismatch reflects a little and lets the rest continue deeper.

  • The skin–air mismatch is so large that essentially no sound crosses it. A dry probe produces a blank screen, not a faint image, which is why gel is needed.
  • Why bowel gas defeats you. Gas reflects nearly everything at its surface, leaving nothing to image the tissue behind it.
  • Why the liver images so well. Homogeneous soft tissue with small internal mismatches lets the beam travel deep while still generating the fine mid-gray texture that lets you notice when a patch of it looks wrong.

Brightness on the screen therefore means echo strength, nothing more. "Hyperechoic" means more sound came back from there than from the tissue around it. It does not mean solid, dense, or hard; a hemangioma and a rib both look bright for entirely different reasons.

Check yourselfA patient's liver looks uniformly bright and you cannot see the diaphragm at all. Name two quite different explanations.

Overall gain set too high, you have amplified everything, including noise, and lost the deep signal in the glare. Fixable in two seconds.

Severe hepatic steatosis, fat attenuates the beam, so genuinely little sound reaches the diaphragm to return. Not fixable, and it matters clinically: this study has reduced sensitivity for focal lesions and should be scored as limited.

Before you accept a bright liver as pathology, turn the gain down and look again. Before you accept it as a gain problem, check whether the deep vasculature has also disappeared.

02Frequency

Higher-frequency sound has a shorter wavelength, and shorter wavelengths resolve finer detail. But higher-frequency sound is also absorbed faster as it travels, so it does not reach as deep. You cannot have both.

Resolution itself has two directions, and they behave differently:

TypeWhat it meansGoverned by
AxialSeparating two structures lying one behind the other, along the beamPulse length, improves with higher frequency
LateralSeparating two structures lying side by side, across the beamBeam width, improves at the focal zone, degrades with depth
Working rule Use the lowest frequency that still gives you an interpretable image at the depth you need. Reaching for more resolution than your depth allows just produces a sharper picture of nothing.

Notes about portable ultrasound devices Battery life is the constraint that will actually interrupt in clinic. Charge the probe and the tablet overnight, carry a power bank, and close the app between patients rather than leaving it live.

Reprocessing/cleaning between patients

Transabdominal scanning on intact skin is a non-critical contact. The requirement is low-level disinfection: remove all gel first (disinfectant will not work through a gel layer), then wipe with a manufacturer-approved agent. No autoclaving, no water rinse. Hand hygiene between patients as normal. Check the approved-agent list for each specific device, the wrong chemical can degrade and damage the probe.

Images, identification, and data

When scanning real patients each stored image must be attached to the correct patient identifier, and every image leaving the device must be handled under your program's de-identification policy. A misattributed image is a patient-safety event, and an ultrasound image is confidential clinical data in exactly the way a written note is.

04The four controls that matter

Modern machines have many controls. Four of them account for nearly all the difference between a diagnostic study and a poor one.

Depth

Set it so the region of interest fills most of the screen without clipping anything relevant. Too much depth shrinks the liver into the top third and wastes both screen resolution and frame rate; too little cuts off the deep segments, where, inconveniently, lesions like to hide. Adjust depth first, before you touch anything else.

Gain and time-gain compensation

These are frequently confused and do different jobs. Overall gain amplifies every returning echo equally; it brightens the whole image, including the noise. Time-gain compensation amplifies selectively by depth, correcting for the fact that deeper echoes have traveled further and lost more energy on the way. TGC can, in most cases, be automatically set on modern machines. On the Vscan it is automatically set as long as "Auto Optimize" is enabled.

The over-gaining trap Excessive gain fills anechoic structures with speckle. A simple cyst acquires internal "debris"; a small hypoechoic lesion is washed into the background. Over-gaining does not just make images ugly, it makes them falsely negative. When in doubt, gain down until the vessels look properly black, then come back up slightly.

Focus

The focal zone is where the beam is narrowest and lateral resolution best. The focus on most modern machines is automatically set. On the Vscan it is automatically set. Rarely, some older machines may require you to set the focus manually.

Harmonics

Tissue harmonic imaging listens at multiples of the transmitted frequency, which suppresses near-field clutter and improves contrast. In practice it helps in patients with a difficult body habitus, which, in a screening population, can be a substantial fraction of your patients. Like many of the other settings, this is also automatically set on most modern machines according to the predefined scanning presets (such as the Abdomen or Cardiac presets).

Doppler angle

This is the angle between the beam and the direction of flow, and it is important to ensure the angle is correct to get a valid Doppler measurement. At 90 degrees there is no detectable shift at all; as the angle narrows toward parallel, accuracy improves sharply.

The working rule is to keep the angle at 60 degrees or less. Beyond 60 the error in any velocity you calculate grows steeply, and a number quoted from a 75-degree angle is not a measurement so much as a guess with decimal places. Steer the color box or angle the probe rather than accepting a poor measurement.

For screening you are mostly asking a yes/no question, is there flow, and which way is it going, rather than measuring velocities. But the same geometry also determines whether you see flow at all, which is why a vessel that appears empty on color may simply be perpendicular to your beam.

05Artifacts you will encounter

Artifacts are a common occurrence, and there are numerous different types and etiologies. Two of the five below are among the most useful diagnostic signs available to you on B-mode.

Artifact 01Posterior acoustic enhancement, useful

Fluid attenuates very little, so the beam emerges from a cyst carrying more energy than the beam either side of it. The tissue directly behind therefore appears brighter.

Use it: enhancement behind a rounded anechoic structure is strong supporting evidence that it really is fluid, not a hypoechoic solid mass. In a cirrhotic liver, that distinction is the difference between reassurance and a recall.

Artifact 02Acoustic shadowing, useful

A strongly reflective or attenuating structure, a gallstone, a calcification, a rib, gas, blocks nearly all sound, leaving an anechoic band deep to it.

Use it: clean posterior shadowing strongly favors a stone over a polyp. Both may be echogenic and sit against the wall, but small stones may not shadow, so confirm with mobility on repositioning as well.

Beware: shadowing from ribs and bowel gas is the mechanism by which liver goes unimaged. Recognizing it is also recognizing that you need a different window.

Artifact 03Reverberation, recognize and move on

Sound bounces repeatedly between two strong reflectors, and each return arrives later than the last. The machine, assuming every echo came from a single round trip, plots them as a series of equally spaced parallel lines going deeper.

Practical significance: near-field reverberation clutter degrades the top of your image. Harmonic imaging exists largely to reduce it.

Artifact 04Mirror image, the one that fools people

The diaphragm is a large, curved, highly reflective surface. Sound can bounce off it, strike a structure, return to the diaphragm, and only then come back to the probe. That extra journey makes the echo late, so the machine places a duplicate of the structure beyond the diaphragm, apparently inside the chest.

Why it matters here: if you have not met this artifact, you may report liver tissue or a lesion above the diaphragm when none exists, or conversely dismiss genuine thoracic pathology as artifact. If a "lesion" sits above the diaphragm and mirrors something below it, angle the probe and watch it move in tandem, duplication of corresponding subdiaphragmatic anatomy that changes with probe angle supports a mirror artifact, but real thoracic pathology can also exist and should not be assumed away without confirming the duplication behavior directly.

Artifact 05Slice thickness, why a simple cyst with debris

The beam has thickness perpendicular to the imaging plane. Near the edge of a cyst, the slice includes both cyst fluid and adjacent liver, and the machine averages them into one plane, producing low-level echoes that look like internal debris.

The habit to build now Any finding seen in only one plane, from only one window, is a candidate artifact until proven otherwise. Confirming a lesion in a second orthogonal plane costs about ten seconds and prevents both false positives and the erosion of confidence that follows a few of them.

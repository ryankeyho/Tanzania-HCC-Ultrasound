Physics & the Vscan Air CL

By the end of this session you should be able to

  • Understand the basic physics of ultrasound
  • Set the appropriate preset, depth, gain and TGC on the Vscan Air CL to get a diagnostic liver image
  • Recognize that you will encounter artifacts during scanning and how they can help aid in recognizing the etiology of potential lesions

01Core concepts of ultrasound

  • The ultrasound probe sends pulses and essentially listens to receive them back and determine the image you receive:
    • The machine places each echo by how long it took to return, assuming sound travels at a fixed speed in soft tissue. Depth on the screen is calculated.
  • Echoes are produced from boundaries between tissues:
    • Gas and bone reflect almost everything, so you cannot see behind them. This same concept explains why you need gel between the probe and the skin, and why bowel gas and ribs block your view.
  • Brightness correlates to echo strength:
    • Hyperechoic and hypoechoic are comparisons, i.e. brighter or darker than the tissue around the structure. Anechoic means no detectable echoes inside it at appropriate settings. Bright does not mean solid or hard, which can be a common misconception.
  • Higher frequency produces a “sharper” image but is limited due to it reaching a shallower depth:
    • The low-frequency curved end reaches the whole liver while the high-frequency linear end only reaches the surface.
  • The beam is narrowest near its focus and diverges beyond it:
    • Past the focal region the beam spreads, so detail is lost. This is one reason a lesion at 15 cm can look blurrier than the same lesion at 5 cm.
Check yourselfA liver looks uniformly bright and you cannot see the diaphragm. Name two different explanations.

Gain may be set too high resulting in increased noise. The first step you should take is to adjust the gain to see if it helps.

In the case of a severely fatty liver the fat absorbs the beam, so less sound reaches the diaphragm. Given this is an inherent patient factor, it is not fixable on your end and you should note that the study is less sensitive for focal lesions.

03The controls, in order

Set them in this order every time and make it second nature.

  1. Preset: Choose the abdominal preset using the curved end of the probe. This allows the Vscan to load the preset focus and harmonics.
  2. Depth: Increase or decrease depth so that the whole organ or structures of interest fill the screen. You can also adjust as needed later on.
  3. Gain: Increase or decrease the gain as needed. You can observe structures such as the hepatic veins to get an index of the appropriate level, as veins should appear black on the screen. If the gain is too high or too low you may miss subtle hyperechoic or hypoechoic lesions.
  1. TGC: Can be turned on as needed to fix depth-related darkening. Essentially, use TGC when deeper structures appear progressively darker or fainter than superficial layers, which occurs because sound energy is lost over distance.
  1. Color: Press the color icon and drag the box over the vessel. You can then adjust the color gain if the color goes outside the vessel of interest. Red means flow toward the probe and blue means flow away (“BART”: blue away, red towards). Red does not mean artery, which is a common misconception. It is also important to note that a vessel lying across the beam can look empty even with normal flow, which is why it is important to view structures in two planes.
  1. Freeze and store: Store still images and short clips, attached to the right patient.

04Artifacts in the liver

Artifacts are not errors that the operator creates but are by-products of the ultrasound itself. However, they do help you decide what a structure is.

ArtifactWhat you seeWhat to do with it
Posterior enhancementBright appearance behind a structureIndicates presence of fluid and supports finding of a cyst
Acoustic shadowingA darkness behind a structureCan support presence of stones or other obstruction. May indicate rib shadowing as well, depending on probe location.
Edge shadowingThin dark lines along the sides of a cyst, vessel or gallbladderCan be seen commonly along the sides of a fluid-filled cyst
ReverberationEvenly spaced bright lines going deeperNothing
Mirror imageA mirror image of the liver above the diaphragmDo not report a lesion above the diaphragm until you have checked it is not a mirror of one below.

Source:[104]

Important! Anything seen in only one plane is a possible artifact. Confirm every finding in a second plane before you record it.

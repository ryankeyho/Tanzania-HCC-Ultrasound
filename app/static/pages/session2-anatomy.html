Liver Anatomy and Landmarks

Session 1 gave you an overview of physics and the machine, and how an image is produced. Before you start scanning the liver systematically, you need the anatomy that lets you say where a finding is, because a lesion recorded as “in the right lobe” cannot reliably be found again, and interval comparison is the mechanism by which surveillance works.

This is a short session. The technique that uses it, controls, probe movements, positioning and the full protocol, is Session 2b, immediately after.

By the end of this session you should be able to

  • Name the eight Couinaud segments and the planes that define them
  • Distinguish portal from hepatic veins at a glance, and use that to orient yourself
  • Locate a finding by segment, so it can be found again on the next scan
  • Recognize the venous star and know what it tells you about your sweep

01Segmental anatomy

Couinaud divides the liver into eight functionally independent segments. The three hepatic veins define vertical planes, and the portal venous bifurcation defines a transverse plane through the middle.

A lesion recorded as "in the right lobe" cannot reliably be found again on the next scan or matched to a lesion on CT. If possible, a lesion recorded as "segment VI, 14 mm" can. Interval comparison is the mechanism by which surveillance works.

02Telling the two venous systems apart

Portal veinsHepatic veins
WallsBright echogenic sheathNo discrete wall
CourseRun with artery and bile duct from the porta hepatisConverge on the IVC toward the diaphragm
BranchingRadiate outward from the hilumRun between segments, toward the heart

The echogenic sheath is the tell. Portal branches carry a conspicuous fibrous covering that reflects strongly; hepatic veins have walls too, but lack that conspicuous echogenic fibrous sheath, so they read as comparatively bare against parenchyma. Once you see the difference, it orients you well, because hepatic veins converging means you are near the dome, and a bright-walled triad means you are near the hilum.

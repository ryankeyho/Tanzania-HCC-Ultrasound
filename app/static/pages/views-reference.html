Required Views by Organ

Every view the screening protocol requires, with the patient position, the probe movement, and the breathing instruction that makes it work. A view you skipped is a region you did not image.

This is a checklist, not a teaching page, the reasoning behind each view lives in Sessions 2, 2a, 3, and 4a. Print it, or keep it open on the tablet next to the scanning app.

The breathing rule, in one line Deep inspiration for anything subcostal, it pushes the diaphragm down and carries the liver below the ribs. Quiet breathing or gentle inspiration for intercostal views over liver dome and spleen, a full breath drags aerated lung down across the window and blanks it out. Quiet breathing for near-field linear-probe work, where movement matters more than access.

The common instinct is "take a deep breath in and hold," applied everywhere, including the views it ruins.
The other rule: sweep, do not photograph Almost nothing on this page is a single still image. Each entry is a sweep, a fan or a rock through the whole structure. A frame is what you store; the sweep is what actually images the tissue. If your stored series has one frame per region, you moved too fast to have covered anything between them.

LIVER, TRANSVERSE 3 columns

Work the liver in three vertical columns, left to right, left lobe, mid, right lobe. Each column runs the same way:

  1. Place the probe as high on the abdomen as it will go, tucked under the costal margin.
  2. Fan upward (cranially) first, this is what catches the top of the liver, the part that sits up under the diaphragm and is otherwise missed entirely.
  3. Fan back down through neutral.
  4. Then step the probe caudally and repeat, working down the column until you run off the inferior edge of the liver.
  5. Move to the next column and start again from the top.
Fan up before you go down The single most common error is starting at a comfortable mid-abdominal position and working downward from there. Everything above the starting point is then never imaged, and that is the dome, where lesions hide. Start high, fan cranially, then descend.

LIVER, LONGITUDINAL 1 continuous sweep

One movement, not a set of stills. Start on the patient's left, probe sagittal, then gently rock the probe up and down (cranial–caudal within the scan plane) while sliding steadily across to the right. The rock is what covers the craniocaudal extent at each position; the slide is what carries you across the organ. Do one without the other and you leave gaps.

GALLBLADDER 2 sweeps, 2 positions

Both planes are sweeps, not single views. In long axis, fan from one wall through to the other so the entire gallbladder passes through the beam; in transverse, sweep from fundus through body to neck. A stone sitting between two static planes is a stone you did not find.

Long axis, fanned wall to wallSupine · inspiration, holdFundus, body, infundibulum; the whole lumen passed through the beam

Patient must be fasted. A contracted post-prandial gallbladder cannot exclude stones, and its wall will look falsely thickened.

BILE DUCT both planes, 3 segments

See it in transverse and long axis, both, every time. Transverse gives you the “Mickey Mouse” arrangement at the porta and the duct in cross-section within the pancreatic head; long axis is where you measure. A duct seen in only one plane is a duct you have not confirmed.

SPLEEN 4 views

Breathing here works the opposite way from the liver You were right to question a deep breath, but the culprit is lung, not bowel. On a full inspiration the aerated lung descends across the intercostal window and wipes out the spleen entirely; this is the same "curtain" effect used deliberately in lung ultrasound.

Use quiet breathing or a gentle half-breath. A little inspiration helps by dropping the spleen clear of the ribs; a full one costs you the whole organ. If the image vanishes as the patient breathes in, that is the curtain, and the instruction to give is "smaller breath."

VESSELS 6 views

01Counting up

Thirty-one entries, but several are continuous sweeps rather than discrete stills, and many structures are captured in passing. A fluent operator gets through the whole study in six to eight minutes. Taking fifteen minutes at first is normal; taking three means something has been skipped, almost always the cranial fan at the top of each liver column.

If a view cannot be obtained Record which one and why. "Pancreatic tail not visualized due to bowel gas" is a useful sentence in a report.

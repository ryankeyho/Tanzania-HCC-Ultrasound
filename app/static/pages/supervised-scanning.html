Weeks 3–5

Peer OSCE Scanning

Ten structured peer scans, not clinical scanning on patients. Each one asks you to find and correctly demonstrate the same set of core anatomy, on a different volunteer every time.

What this block requires

  • 10 peer OSCE scans, each on a different peer volunteer, each observed and scored
  • On every scan, correctly locate and demonstrate: the liver (complete systematic survey), the gallbladder, the pancreas, the major vessels (aorta, IVC, portal vein), and the spleen
  • A structured report on every scan, using the Session 6 fields
  • OSCE scoring applied to each of the 10 scans

01Peer scanning

This program does not place trainees on real patients as part of the course itself. Competency is assessed instead through structured peer scanning: trainees scan each other, under observation, using the same protocol taught in Sessions 2a, 2b, and the simulation lab.

Ten scans will be done on ten different people, not one. This provides variation in body habitus and anatomy, and tests the habits you have developed, your performance, and your overall understanding of the course material and ultrasound use.

0210 scans

Each of the 10 scans is a complete encounter, not a single-organ station. The same five targets are assessed every time:

TargetWhat counts as found appropriately
LiverFull systematic survey per the Session 2b protocol, three transverse columns with a cranial fan each, longitudinal sweep, intercostal, reposition. Not a single static image.
GallbladderLong axis, fanned wall to wall, plus a transverse sweep fundus to neck. Identified and correctly named, not just visualized incidentally.
PancreasLocated via the splenic vein landmark from Session 4a, head through body toward the tail.
VesselsAorta and IVC in transverse and longitudinal, plus the portal vein at the porta hepatis.
SpleenLeft lateral intercostal approach, true long axis between the poles.

A scan that finds four of five targets but skips one is an incomplete scan, not a partial pass on that target. Report what could not be found and why, the same honesty standard as any real study: a non-visualized structure that is documented as not visualized is a limited scan, correctly reported as such. A non-visualized structure that goes unmentioned is a failure.

03How a peer OSCE scan runs

  1. Pair up. One trainee scans, one is the volunteer. An examiner observes.
  2. Set up the machine in the fixed order from Session 2b before touching the volunteer.
  3. Work through all five targets in a fixed order: liver, gallbladder, pancreas, vessels, spleen.
  4. Store and label a representative image for each target found.
  5. Write the structured report before discussing the scan with the examiner.
  6. Examiner scores the scan, then debriefs.
  7. Switch roles or rotate to a new volunteer. Repeat until 10 scored scans are complete.
Write the report before the discussion It is tempting to talk the scan through with the examiner and then write. Do it the other way round. The report you write, unaided by others, is one of the things being assessed.

04Competency assessment

Each of the 10 scans is scored on the OSAUS scale, seven domains, each scored 1 to 5, total out of 35:

DomainAssesses
Indication for the examinationDo you know what this scan is for?
Applied knowledge of equipmentProbe choice, presets, controls
Image optimizationDepth, gain applied deliberately and correctly
Systematic examinationAll five targets covered, in order, completely
Image interpretationRecognizing what is on the screen
DocumentationThe structured report and stored images
Medical decision-makingWhat you would do next, given what you found

Assessments are recorded on the OSCE Checklist, which totals automatically across all 10 scans.

05Limitations of peer scanning

A peer volunteer is healthy. You will likely not find a real hemangioma, a real cyst, or a real cirrhotic liver in this exercise, and peer scanning is not designed to teach lesion detection. What it tests is technique: whether you can systematically and reliably find normal anatomy, under observation, on a body you have not scanned before. That is a necessary and separate skill from interpreting what you find, which Sessions 3 through 6 and the simulation lab's injected phantom nodules are what actually teach.

Treat a peer scan that goes well as a display of technique, which can then be applied to real-world scanning.

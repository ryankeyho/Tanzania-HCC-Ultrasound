02The reality of US screening for HCC

What a negative screen means Ultrasound sensitivity for early-stage HCC is limited, well under 50% in some series. A negative screen means no lesion was seen during that screen. It does not necessarily exclude early disease. This is why fixed interval screening matters.

The phrase to avoid in reports and in communication with other providers as well as the patient is "no evidence of malignancy." It overstates what the study can truly establish. "No focal lesion seen in the visualized liver" is a more accurate statement after a negative study.

Likewise, AFP alone is not adequate as a surveillance test, sensitivity and specificity are both insufficient. It is used alongside imaging, not instead of it. A rising AFP trend, for example doubling, or increases on two consecutive measurements per the selected protocol, warrants evaluation even when the absolute value remains below the threshold.

03Regional importance of screening

Three things distinguish HCC in sub-Saharan Africa from other countries:

  • Chronic HBV dominates. Unlike other settings where HCV, alcohol, and metabolic disease drive a significant proportion of cases, HBV is the principal etiology, in addition to aflatoxin exposure acting synergistically to raise risk further.
  • Onset is often earlier. Patients present at younger ages than the surveillance literature typically assumes.
  • Presentation is later in the disease. A larger proportion present with advanced stage, non-curable disease, which indicates a gap in disease screening.

HBV causes HCC without requiring cirrhosis, identifying carriers is itself a screening activity.

04Findings outside the liver

Sessions 2a and 4a train you to recognize abnormality beyond the liver, an aneurysmal aorta, a double duct sign, hydronephrosis, a suspicious node. That creates an obligation. A program that teaches its operators to notice things and then gives them nowhere to send them has recreated, on a smaller scale, exactly the failure this session warns about: findings generated with no pathway to act on them.

So decide these before the first patient is scanned, not at the moment you meet one.

Two principles that keep this manageable One: an incidental finding is documented and routed, never worked up by the screening operator. Your job ends at recognition and referral.

Two: extrahepatic findings must not displace the liver study. If the pancreas took the time the hepatic dome needed, the study failed at its actual purpose, and a limited liver exam with a well-documented aorta is still a limited liver exam.

05Task-shifting

The case for training non-radiologist clinicians to perform screening ultrasound is capacity: the population needing surveillance substantially exceeds available radiologist and sonographer time. It is not that non-specialists scan better, and not that specialist input becomes unnecessary.

What a task-shifted program requires to be safe:

RequirementWhy
Defined referral pathway, established firstScreening that generates findings nobody can act on can be worse than not screening
Structured reportingMakes results interpretable by clinicians who did not perform the scan
Expert over-read of stored imagesThe mechanism by which quality is maintained after training ends
Competence assessment, not just scan countsVolume alone does not establish that someone can scan safely
Program indicatorsAdequacy rate, proportion of positives completing diagnostic imaging, interval adherence
The indicator that matters most Not the number of scans performed, the proportion of positive screens that complete diagnostic imaging. A program scanning thousands with no downstream completion produces no benefit at all. If you track one number, track that one.

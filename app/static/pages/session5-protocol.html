The cirrhosis, noncirrhotic HBV, and advanced-fibrosis criteria are supported by the 2023 AASLD HCC guidance; HBV coinfection and HBsAg-loss criteria by the 2025 AASLD/IDSA guideline; and post-SVR surveillance by AASLD HCC and HCV guidance.

Footnotes

  • For people with chronic HBV from Africa, AASLD states surveillance can begin as early as the third decade of life. Guidelines do not make age 20 an absolute requirement for everyone.
  • PAGE-B ≥10 is included in AASLD criteria, but PAGE-B was validated primarily in patients on antiviral therapy.
  • HBV/HDV, HBV/HIV, HBV/HCV, and post-HBsAg-loss surveillance recommendations in the 2025 guideline are conditional, based on very-low-certainty evidence.

03What a negative scan means

According to AASLD, ultrasound alone detects approximately 47% of early-stage hepatocellular carcinomas (HCC), increasing to 63% when AFP is added. A negative ultrasound (US-1) means no observations or only definitely benign observations were identified. A negative scan does not exclude HCC. When visualization is adequate (VIS-A or VIS-B) and AFP is not elevated, continue surveillance with ultrasound and AFP every six months.

STANDARDIZED REPORTING — ACR LI-RADS

Report both the ultrasound surveillance category (US-1, US-2, or US-3) and the visualization score (VIS-A, VIS-B, or VIS-C).

Example: “US-1 Negative. VIS-A: No or minimal visualization limitations. Repeat surveillance ultrasound in six months.”

Important: Severe visualization limitations (VIS-C) warrant repeat ultrasound within three months or consideration of alternative surveillance imaging, particularly when poor visualization is likely to persist.

AFP

AFP is used alongside ultrasound, not as a replacement. AFP ≥20 ng/mL or rising AFP warrants diagnostic multiphasic contrast-enhanced CT or MRI, even when ultrasound is negative. Concerning trends include AFP doubling or increases on two consecutive measurements, although the optimal use of longitudinal AFP trends requires further validation.

04HCC in sub-Saharan Africa

  • Hepatitis B is the leading cause of HCC in SSA. In the Africa Liver Cancer Consortium, which included Tanzania, HBV accounted for 55% of HCC cases with available etiologic data outside Egypt. HBV can cause HCC without cirrhosis, making identification of chronic HBV infection essential for determining eligibility for HCC surveillance.
  • Aflatoxin amplifies HCC risk. Aflatoxins, which can contaminate maize and groundnuts, are established human carcinogens. A prospective cohort study in China demonstrated a synergistic association: HCC risk was approximately 7-fold higher with HBV alone, 3-fold with aflatoxin exposure alone, and 59-fold with both, compared with neither exposure.
  • Patients present at younger ages. Median age at HCC diagnosis was 46 years in African countries outside Egypt, compared with 58 years in Egypt.
  • Late presentation and limited treatment are major challenges. Approximately 95% of patients outside Egypt presented with advanced or terminal-stage HCC, and only 3% received HCC-directed treatment in the consortium. Earlier detection through surveillance, coupled with accessible diagnostic and treatment pathways, is critical to improving outcomes.

05Findings outside the liver

During liver ultrasound surveillance, clinically significant abnormalities outside the liver may be identified. These findings should be documented, and directed to an appropriate clinical pathway.

FindingUrgencyReferral pathway
Double duct sign (dilated CBD and pancreatic duct)Prompt evaluation for possible pancreaticobiliary obstruction or malignancy. Emergency evaluation if cholangitis is suspected.Gastroenterology or hepatobiliary referral for CT and/or MRI/MRCP as appropriate.
Dilated CBD with distal duct not visualizedFurther imaging when obstruction is suspected. Nonvisualization alone does not establish obstruction.Local medical or gastroenterology pathway. MRI/MRCP or CT when indicated.
Abdominal aortic aneurysm ≥3 cmSurveillance based on diameter, however there should be urgent evaluation for symptoms attributable to the aneurysm or suspected rupture.Vascular/medical referral. Surveillance every 3 years for 3.0–3.9 cm.
HydronephrosisDepends on symptoms, cause, and renal function. Suspected infected obstruction requires emergency evaluation.Medical/urology referral. Urgent referral for suspected infected obstruction, renal dysfunction, or high-risk findings.
Bulky or suspicious lymphadenopathy associated with a liver lesionFurther evaluation based on clinical and imaging findings.Document as a separate finding within the ultrasound report and include in the liver lesion referral.
Suspected pancreatic massPrompt diagnostic evaluation.Gastroenterology/hepatobiliary referral for CT with contrast or MRI.

  • A limited liver or RUQ ultrasound is not a comprehensive evaluation of other abdominal organs. However, unexpected clinically significant findings require appropriate communication and referral.
  • Additional scanning should not compromise liver visualization, but potentially life-threatening findings take priority over routine surveillance.

06Task-shifting

Training non-radiologist clinicians may expand access to HCC surveillance where specialist imaging capacity is limited. However, expanding scanning capacity requires standardized protocols, demonstrated operator competency, appropriate clinical supervision, quality assurance, and reliable pathways for diagnostic evaluation.

RequirementWhy it matters
An established referral pathwayPatients with suspicious findings must have access to diagnostic imaging and specialist evaluation.
A standardized liver protocol and trained operatorsA dedicated liver surveillance protocol, combined with appropriate training, may improve detection of focal liver observations compared with a general abdominal protocol.
Quality assurance and continued practiceRoutine review of image quality, operator competency, and examination volume supports consistent performance.
Structured reporting and qualified interpretationStandardized US LI-RADS categories and visualization scores with consistent communication and management. Interpretation and supervision should also be provided by appropriately qualified clinicians.
Outpatient surveillance when feasibleACR LI-RADS recommends outpatient examinations because acute illness can impair liver visualization. If outpatient imaging is unavailable, scanning may occur after acute conditions resolve.
Tracking surveillance and diagnostic follow-throughThere needs to be dedicated monitoring of completion of scheduled surveillance intervals, adequate liver visualization on screening US, and appropriate follow-up of abnormal results.
Key takeaway Program success should be measured not only by the number of ultrasound examinations performed, but also by surveillance adherence, examination quality, completion of indicated diagnostic imaging after abnormal results, and eventual appropriate treatment.

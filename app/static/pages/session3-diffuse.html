Diffuse Liver Pathology

Focal lesion detection is the focus of this training; however, the background liver is also important to understand, both for scanning and for surveillance: how likely any lesion is to be malignant, and how much you can trust a negative scan.

By the end of this session you should be able to

  • Distinguish normal, steatotic, and cirrhotic liver on B-mode
  • State how the background liver changes the interpretation of a negative screen

01Normal Liver

Normal liver parenchyma is homogeneous, mid-gray, and either isoechoic with or very slightly brighter than the adjacent right renal cortex. That kidney comparison is your calibration standard, it travels with the patient, so it is more reliable than any impression of absolute brightness, which changes with your gain setting.

Always image liver and kidney together Judge echogenicity on a single image containing both. An impression formed on separate images, at different gain settings, is not an assessment, it is a guess.

02Steatosis

Fat scatters and attenuates the beam. Five findings follow, and the first three progress together as severity increases:

  • Increased echogenicity relative to renal cortex, the earliest and least specific sign
  • Blurred vascular margins (delineation of the vessel walls is lost), the bright walls of portal branches become indistinct against brightened parenchyma
  • Posterior attenuation, the deep liver darkens, and in severe cases the diaphragm disappears entirely
  • Inhomogeneity, the brightening is rarely perfectly even; patchy or geographic variation is part of the pattern, not a reason to doubt it
  • Focal areas of reduced fatty infiltration, the inverse problem. A patch of liver spared from fat sits relatively hypoechoic against the bright background around it and can look like a mass. Session 4 covers this pseudolesion directly; the clue is a geographic, angular shape and a location that favors the gallbladder fossa or segment IV, rather than a rounded mass margin
Why this matters for screening Severe steatosis can degrade the ability to detect focal lesions. The beam is not reaching the deep liver, so a negative result in a severely steatotic liver leads to a higher chance of false negatives.

03Cirrhosis

Steatosis and fibrosis both raise echogenicity, so brightness alone cannot separate them. What distinguishes cirrhosis is shape.

Look for: nodular surface contour; coarse heterogeneous echotexture; lobar redistribution with caudate and left-lobe hypertrophy against right-lobe atrophy; and the signs of portal hypertension below.

05Schistosomal periportal fibrosis

Schistosomiasis produces fibrosis around the portal tracts, not throughout the lobules. On ultrasound this appears as thickened echogenic bands following the portal branches, the "pipe-stem" appearance, with echogenic septa outlining polygonal areas of relatively preserved parenchyma.

The distinction that changes management Periportal fibrosis causes presinusoidal portal hypertension. The patient may have marked splenomegaly, varices, and a dilated portal vein, while liver architecture and surface contour remain largely preserved, and hepatic function stays intact until late.
Do not interpret this as "no HCC risk" Hepatosplenic schistosomiasis typically produces presinusoidal periportal fibrosis with relatively preserved lobular architecture, not classic cirrhosis. Evidence for an independent association between schistosomiasis and HCC risk remains uncertain and confounded, largely by coexisting viral hepatitis in endemic populations; schistosomiasis alone is not an AASLD surveillance indication. Assess HBV and HCV status and evaluate independently for cirrhosis before deciding on surveillance.

06Other patterns worth recognizing

PatternOther etiologies of hepatic congestion that can mimic cirrhosis
You may see dilated hepatic veins and an IVC that does not collapse with respiration. This can be right heart failure, not liver disease. Congestion distends the hepatic venous system, whereas cirrhotic portal hypertension usually distends the portal system.
PatternThe "starry sky" liver pattern seen in acute hepatitis
Parenchymal edema lowers background echogenicity, making the bright portal triads stand out sharply. It is a transient appearance and does not indicate chronic disease.
PatternBudd-Chiari syndrome
Hepatic venous outflow obstruction, with absent or reversed hepatic vein flow and intrahepatic collaterals. The caudate lobe drains directly to the IVC, so it is spared and hypertrophies.
PatternGallbladder wall thickening in cirrhosis
Common, and usually secondary to hypoalbuminemia and portal hypertension rather than cholecystitis. However, this cannot be excluded on imaging alone and must be based on exam, history, and imaging together.

Diffuse Liver Pathology

The background liver decides how likely a lesion is to be malignant and how much you can trust a negative scan. This session covers normal, fatty and cirrhotic liver, portal hypertension, and schistosomal fibrosis, which you will see often in Tanzania.

By the end of this session you should be able to

  • Distinguish normal, fatty and cirrhotic liver on B-mode
  • Recognize the signs of portal hypertension and measure the spleen correctly
  • Recognize schistosomal periportal fibrosis and how it differs from cirrhosis
  • Explain how the background liver changes what a negative scan means

01Normal liver: compare with the kidney

Normal liver is evenly textured and about as bright as the cortex of the right kidney. Judge brightness on one image that contains both, because the kidney is a reference that travels with the patient, while absolute brightness changes with your gain.

Source:[123]

02Fatty liver (steatosis)

The five criteria are:[123]

  • Brighter than the kidney. The earliest sign.
  • Vessel walls fade. The bright portal walls blend into the bright background.
  • Posterior attenuation. The deep liver darkens; in severe cases the diaphragm disappears.
  • Uneven brightness. Patchy variation is part of the pattern.
  • Focal areas spared from fat. These look darker than the bright liver around them and can look like a mass.
Spared areas that look like a mass Focal fatty sparing is usually geographic in shape and sits in typical places: next to the porta hepatis (segment IV) or the gallbladder fossa. A spared area that is round, or outside those places, cannot be assumed benign; spared areas can also surround a real lesion. Treat it as a focal lesion (Session 4).

Source:[124]

Why this matters for screening In a severely fatty liver the beam does not reach the deep liver, so small lesions there can be missed. Record such a study as limited.

03Cirrhosis

Fat and fibrosis both make the liver brighter, so brightness cannot separate them. Shape can.

Checklist for cirrhosis:

  1. Contour. Look at the deep surface with the curved end and the front surface with the linear end.
  2. Lobe proportions. Is the caudate lobe enlarged?
  3. Hepatic veins. Fewer visible veins, or irregular walls?
  4. Portal veins. Abrupt changes in calibre inside the liver?
  5. Ligamentum teres. Has the old umbilical vein reopened, with flow on color Doppler?
  6. Hilum. Increased arterial flow?
  7. Portal hypertension. Ascites, spleen size, portal, superior mesenteric and splenic veins, and collaterals (abdominal wall, mesenteric, splenorenal or gastric, gallbladder bed).
  • Surface nodularity is among the most accurate signs: about 88% sensitive and up to 95% specific.[125]
  • Redistribution of volume: the right lobe shrinks while the caudate and the left lateral segments (II, III) enlarge. A caudate-to-right-lobe width ratio over 0.65 is highly specific but less sensitive .[126]
  • Early cirrhosis can still have a normal-sized or even enlarged liver; shrinkage comes late.[125]

05Schistosomal periportal fibrosis

In hepatosplenic schistosomiasis, eggs lodge in small portal veins and cause a granulomatous reaction that heals as fibrosis around the portal tracts. On ultrasound you see thick, bright bands along the portal branches, a portal vein surrounded by a bright collar of fibrous tissue, and bright septa outlining polygonal areas of fairly normal liver.

How it differs from cirrhosis Patients present with portal hypertension: a large spleen, varices and sometimes ascites. Liver function is usually preserved until late, because the liver cells themselves are not the main target. The liver surface can look irregular, so contour alone can mimic cirrhosis; in one published case, biopsy showed preserved architecture with schistosomal fibrosis.

Schistosomiasis is an important cause of periportal fibrosis and portal hypertension in endemic areas.[127]

Do not read this as “no liver cancer risk” Hepatitis B and C are common in the same populations. Check hepatitis B and C status and assess for cirrhosis before deciding on surveillance (Session 5).

06Other patterns worth recognizing

PatternWhat you see and what it means
Congestion from the heartWide hepatic veins and a full IVC can suggest raised right-sided pressure or venous congestion. Interpret them with Doppler, the heart and the clinical picture; they do not exclude liver disease, and congestion and cirrhosis can occur together.
“Starry sky”A darker liver with bright portal walls standing out, described in acute hepatitis. The sign is neither sensitive nor specific.
Budd–Chiari syndromeBlocked hepatic vein outflow. The caudate lobe drains straight into the IVC through its own veins, so it is spared and enlarges.
Thick gallbladder wallCommon with ascites, heart failure, hepatitis and after a meal, so wall thickening alone is nonspecific. A positive sonographic Murphy sign supports acute cholecystitis, but its absence does not exclude it.

Do it on the Vscan

  1. Store one image with the liver and right kidney side by side. Is the liver brighter?
  2. Switch to the linear end and store an image of the liver surface. Smooth or irregular?
  3. Measure the spleen’s length and check it against the height table or SplenoCalc.
  4. Measure the portal vein at the hilum.

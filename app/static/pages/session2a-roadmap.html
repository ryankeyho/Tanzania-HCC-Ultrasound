The Upper Abdominal Roadmap

By the end of this session you should be able to

  • Identify the aorta, IVC, celiac trunk, SMA, splenic vein and portal confluence on a transverse epigastric view
  • Measure the aorta correctly and recognize an aneurysm
  • Check the standard free-fluid spaces and recognize the spine sign

02The transverse epigastric view

Place the probe in the transverse position just below the xiphoid with the marker pointing to the patient's right. Make sure to set the depth and gain appropriately.

  1. Spine. A bright curved line with dense shadow behind it. Everything else is anterior.
  2. Aorta. Just anterior to the vertebral body, on the patient's left. Round, thick-walled, pulsatile.
  3. IVC. To the patient's right of the aorta. Less round, and it changes with breathing.
  4. Celiac trunk. Slide slightly caudal until it branches into the common hepatic and splenic arteries.
  5. SMA. A little further caudal: a small circle anterior to the aorta.
  6. Splenic vein and confluence. Follow the splenic vein, which runs anterior to the SMA, to the right until it meets the superior mesenteric vein. The portal vein begins here.
Aorta or IVC? Identify the vessel by its course, its relationship to the aorta and liver, and its behavior with breathing. The aorta is round, pulsatile and sits just in front of the spine. The IVC lies to the patient’s right, runs up into the liver and changes size with respiration. Compression alone is not a reliable way to tell them apart.

03Normal values

You will not measure all of these routinely, but it is good to have a general idea of the approximate normal so you can recognize abnormalities in the future.

Abdominal aortaAbout 2 cm is typical; 3 cm or more is an aneurysm

Important incidental finding An abdominal aorta of 3 cm or more is an aneurysm. Most abdominal aneurysms are below the renal arteries, so follow the aorta all the way to the bifurcation. Record the diameter and refer per your local pathway. Thresholds for the iliac arteries differ from the aorta, so do not apply the 3 cm rule to them.

Measuring the aorta

  • Measure perpendicular to the vessel.
  • In long axis, stay in the center of the vessel.
  • Include any mural thrombus. (Thrombus can look like the wall and make the aorta seem smaller than it is.)
  • State the method you used for measurement, for example outer wall to outer wall, or leading edge to leading edge. Programs differ, so record which one.
  • Expect some variation. (The diameter changes by up to about 5 mm between systole and diastole.)

04Getting your orientation prior to every scan

Important to do before any abdominal scan to ensure you have your landmarks and orientation correct.

  1. Transverse epigastric view: spine, aorta, IVC, celiac trunk, SMA.
  2. Follow the splenic vein to the right, with the pancreas lying over it, to the confluence.
  3. Slide caudally along the aorta to the bifurcation, watching the diameter for any obvious aneurysm.
  4. Rotate to long axis over the aorta and IVC.

Store one image of each step. The whole sweep should take under a minute once you have practiced enough.

05Free fluid

Ascites is common in the cirrhotic patients you will screen, so you will see free fluid often. Simple ascites is usually anechoic, and complex fluid can contain echoes, debris, or septations. Free fluid usually takes the shape of the space it sits in. One obvious marker of free fluid is that it will often form a triangle in the space where two organs meet.

WindowWhere to look
Right upper quadrantHepatorenal space (Morison's pouch), the caudal tip of the liver, and above the liver under the diaphragm. Include the inferior liver tip, where small amounts of free fluid may be detected.
Left upper quadrantMost often around the spleen, between the spleen and the diaphragm, and at the spleen tip. Inspect the perisplenic and subdiaphragmatic spaces, the inferior splenic tip, and the splenorenal recess.
PelvisIn men, the rectovesical pouch, behind the bladder. In women, the pouch of Douglas (rectouterine pouch), which lies posterior to the uterus rather than immediately behind the bladder. A small amount of pelvic fluid can be physiological, particularly in reproductive-age women.

Source:[106]

The spine sign

Normally you will see the spine seem to stop at the diaphragm, because aerated lung blocks the view above it, and a mirror image of the liver appears there instead. If you can see the spine continuing above the diaphragm, something other than aerated lung is carrying sound there. This usually means fluid of some sort, but consolidated lung can do the same.

06A note on eFAST

Extended Focused Assessment with Sonography in Trauma (eFAST) is a trauma protocol that looks for free fluid in the abdomen, chest, and pericardium, and for pneumothorax. It is not the focus of this course, but it is important to know how to do it, as the abdominal windows are the same ones that you use for ascites in this course as well.

Limitations How much fluid you can detect depends on the view, where the fluid has collected and the operator. Small amounts are sometimes visible, particularly in the pelvis. The important point is that ultrasound does not tell you where fluid came from or whether it is blood or ascites. A negative scan does not exclude a problem. However, in your screening population fluid usually means ascites.

The Upper Abdominal Roadmap

If you start by looking for the liver, you may end up lost or with an incomplete image of the liver. Starting from the vessels means always knowing your position, because the great vessels are constant, high-contrast, and lead directly to every organ you care about.

This is not a general abdominal ultrasound course, and you are not being trained to report the bowel or the pelvis. What you need is enough orientation to find the liver reliably, enough recognition to know when something outside the liver is clearly abnormal, and enough judgment to escalate it rather than ignore it.

By the end of this session you should be able to

  • Identify aorta, IVC, celiac trunk, SMA, splenic vein and the portal confluence on a transverse epigastric view
  • Use the splenic vein to find the pancreas, and the confluence to find the portal vein
  • State normal calibers for the vessels you will measure
  • Recognize an abdominal aortic aneurysm as an incidental finding and know what to do with it
  • Perform a short orientation sweep before every liver study

01Why start with vessels

Three reasons, and they compound:

  • They are unmistakable. Anechoic, tubular, pulsatile or compressible. Even on a poor image, you can find the aorta.
  • They are fixed. Liver size, shape and echotexture vary enormously between patients. The aorta is always anterior to the spine and the IVC is always to its right.
  • They lead everywhere. The splenic vein finds the pancreas. The confluence finds the portal vein. The portal vein finds the porta hepatis, and with it the bile duct and hepatic artery. One landmark chain, and you have the entire upper abdomen.
The habit worth building Start every abdominal study with a transverse epigastric view and identify the aorta before you do anything else. It takes ten seconds and it means you are never scanning blind.

02The transverse epigastric view

Probe transverse, just below the xiphoid, marker to the patient's right. Angle slightly cranially and you will see the vascular arrangement that organizes everything else.

Work through it in this order

  1. Find the spine, a bright curved surface with dense shadowing behind. Everything else is anterior to it.
  2. Aorta, immediately anterior to the vertebral body, on the patient's left of midline. Thick-walled, pulsatile, does not compress.
  3. IVC, to the patient's right of the aorta. Thinner-walled, varies with respiration, compresses with probe pressure.
  4. Celiac trunk, the seagull, arising anteriorly.
  5. Slide caudally a centimeter to the SMA and its fat collar.
  6. Find the splenic vein crossing transversely, and follow it to the right.
  7. The confluence, where splenic vein meets superior mesenteric vein. The portal vein begins here.
Aorta or IVC? If you are unsure, press. The IVC compresses under moderate probe pressure and the aorta does not. Color Doppler settles it too, but compression is faster and needs no settings change.

03Normal calibers

You will not measure most of these routinely. Know them so that a grossly abnormal one registers as abnormal rather than passing unnoticed.

The one incidental finding you must not walk past An infrarenal aorta of 3 cm or more is an aneurysm. You are scanning the upper abdomen of middle-aged and older adults, many with vascular risk factors, so you will eventually find one. It is not what you came for, and it still needs referral. Measure outer wall to outer wall on a true transverse section, record it, and refer per local pathway.

04Other branches

Three more vessels are worth being able to find. None is a screening target in liver scanning, but each is a landmark or an incidental you should not misread.

VesselWhereWhy this is important
Renal arteriesArising laterally from the aorta just below the SMA. Best seen on a transverse plane at that level, or in long axis from a 45-degree oblique with the patient supine or in left lateral decubitus.They mark the level that divides suprarenal from infrarenal aorta, which is what makes an aortic measurement interpretable
Inferior mesenteric arterySmall, lumen only 2–4 mm. Arises anterolaterally to the left, roughly 4–5 cm above the aortic bifurcation, and runs toward the left hip.Rarely needed but worth recognizing if possible so you do not mistake it for something else while sweeping the aorta
Gastroduodenal arteryBranch of the common hepatic, running along the anterior surface of the pancreatic head.A useful landmark for the pancreatic head, and a structure not to confuse with a dilated bile duct
Measuring the aorta properly Measure on a true orthogonal section, perpendicular to the vessel axis, an oblique cut through a normal aorta reads falsely wide, which is the single most common technical error in aortic measurement. Use outer wall to outer wall as the usual screening standard, and say so when you record it; inner-to-inner reads a few millimeters smaller.

The aorta also pulses, so its diameter varies by up to about 5 mm between systole and diastole. About 90% of aneurysms are infrarenal, which is one more reason the renal arteries above matter.

05The orientation sweep

Thirty to sixty seconds, before the liver protocol proper. It confirms your bearings and catches gross extrahepatic abnormalities.

Four steps

  1. Transverse epigastric. Spine, aorta, IVC, celiac trunk, SMA.
  2. Follow the splenic vein left to right, noting the pancreas draped over it, to the confluence.
  3. Slide caudally along the aorta to the bifurcation, watching caliber. This is your AAA check.
  4. Rotate to sagittal over the aorta and IVC to confirm both in long axis.

Then begin the liver protocol from Session 2b, already oriented.

06Free fluid, and the eFAST windows

Free fluid is the one extrahepatic finding you will meet often, because ascites is common in the cirrhotic population you are screening. Fluid appears anechoic, conforming to whatever space it sits in, moving with position.

The reason to learn the formal windows is that fluid collects in predictable places, and knowing them means you check the right spots.

You already scan the first two as part of the liver scan. The hepatorenal recess in particular is the most sensitive right-sided window and you are there anyway for the liver–kidney echogenicity comparison.

Free fluid, not just ascites

Ascites is the presentation you will see most often in this population, but the underlying sign is the same wherever fluid collects: an anechoic space taking on the shape of whatever it sits between, often producing a triangular or wedge-shaped pocket where two organs or peritoneal surfaces meet. Learn to recognize this and you will catch fluid and ascites when scanning patients.

LocationWhat to look for
Perihepatic / hepatorenal recessThe classic ascites view; a triangular pocket between liver and kidney
PerisplenicSame principle on the left; fluid outlines the spleen's inferior pole against the kidney
PelvisThe most dependent space in a supine patient; often the first place free fluid appears in small volumes
The spine sign Above the diaphragm, the vertebral bodies are normally hidden by aerated lung, so the spine appears to stop at the diaphragm. If you can see the spine continuing above the diaphragm, fluid is transmitting sound where lung should be, a pleural effusion.

A note on eFAST

These windows come from eFAST, Extended Focused Assessment with Sonography for Trauma, a protocol for finding free fluid and thoracic injury in an unstable trauma patient. The "extended" part means the thorax is included: pneumothorax and hemothorax alongside the abdominal windows.

eFAST is not what this course is focused on; it is mentioned for two reasons. First, the windows are the same ones you use for ascites, so learning them once serves both. Second, a clinician who can scan an abdomen will eventually be asked to look at a patient not only in the context of screening for liver cancer, but for other acute illnesses, so it is worth knowing.

The limits are the important part A negative scan does not exclude injury. Known blind spots include retroperitoneal bleeding, the pancreatic tail, bowel injury, diaphragmatic tears, and fluid volumes too small to see, and free fluid alone cannot distinguish blood from urine or ascites.

The same caution applies in your setting: finding no free fluid does not rule out an abnormality; in a cirrhotic patient, finding fluid usually means ascites rather than anything acute.

Scanning Technique: Controls, Movements and the Protocol

By the end of this session you should be able to

  • Set up the Vscan in a fixed order
  • Perform the five probe movements
  • Instruct patient on positioning and breathing instruction as appropriate
  • Perform a scan of the liver and measure liver size
  • Identify and measure the common bile duct, and find the gallbladder

01Watch first

02Setting up

Set up in the same order every time: probe, preset, depth, gain.

#ControlSet it to
1ProbeThe curved end. The linear end is only for the liver surface (Session 3).
2PresetAbdominal.
3DepthSet it so the structures of interest fill the screen.
4GainVessels black, liver mid-gray.

If the image is still poor, change the window, the patient's position or their breathing before reaching for the controls again.

03The five movements

MovementWhat it doesUse it to
SlideMoves the whole probe across the skin without fanning or rocking.Finding a new window, move to new anatomy, follow a vessel or other possible abnormality.
Tilt (fan)Angles the direction of the beam side to side through the tissue while the probe otherwise stays still.Sweep through an organ which will produce a series of slices. This produces what is called volume and is important in scanning organs as you see when scanning more cranial portions of the liver.
RotateTurns the probe around on its own axis.Allows you to switch between long and short axis. This is important to get the second plane of a finding for lesions, organs, vessels, ducts etc.
RockTips the probe toward or away from the marker, staying in the same plane.Can help bring a structure into the center of the image.
CompressApply pressure with the probe.Helps push bowel gas away which often can obstruct your image. Compression can also help distinguish a vein (compresses) from an artery (does not).
Hand positioning One hand should be on the probe at all times, with the other on the machine (or near the device in the case of the Vscan) to adjust depth, gain, freeze and save as needed.

04Patient position and breathing

Position or instructionWhen it helps
Supine, knees bent, arms at the sidesThe default. Slightly bent knees can help relax the abdominal wall.
Deep breath and holdBrings the liver and gallbladder down below the ribs for subcostal views. Also helps in seeing your major abdominal vessels as well as your hilum.
Right arm above the headCan help widen the right intercostal spaces for intercostal views.
Left lateral decubitus (lying on the left side)Brings the gallbladder toward the midline. Also helps you check whether a potential stone moves.
More uprightShifts the liver and gallbladder down when subcostal views fail.

Fanning is important to cover the liver completely in your transverse view, not by sliding to a single frame. In every transverse sweep, fan up toward the diaphragm first. If you start mid-abdomen and work down, the top of the liver is never imaged. Reaching the venous star shows you have reached the top centrally.

Two planes, always Cover every region in two planes, and confirm every finding in both. A finding seen in only one plane may be an artifact.

Liver size

Measure the largest craniocaudal span in the midclavicular line, from the diaphragm to the lower edge. A healthy liver is under about 16 cm. 16 cm or more suggests hepatomegaly. Body build and breath-hold change the number considerably, so measure the same way every time.

06The porta hepatis and the common bile duct

  1. Find the portal triad. Follow the gallbladder neck toward the portal vein, or slide from the epigastrium toward the right. In cross section the portal vein is the face and the hepatic artery and common duct are the two ears: the “Mickey Mouse” sign.
  1. Differentiate the duct from the artery with color Doppler if needed. The artery fills with color and the duct does not.
  2. Rotate the probe to long axis. The duct runs anterior and parallel to the portal vein.
  1. Look for the “olive sandwich.” In long axis, the hepatic artery appears as a small round “olive” in cross section, sandwiched between the bile duct above it and the portal vein below it. This helps confirm you are looking at the duct.
  2. Measure inner wall to inner wall, perpendicular to the duct, in the “Hilum eye” view at the porta hepatis (see the named views below).

What is normal

SituationUpper limit
Adult with a gallbladderAbout 6 to 7 mm. The duct widens by about 1 mm per decade in older adults, so up to about 9 mm can be normal at age 90.
After cholecystectomyMay be larger than 7 mm. Caliber depends on age, cholecystectomy and measurement site, read in the context of symptoms and labs. Unexpected dilation needs further clinical assessment.

Source:[107]

  • Quick check: a duct less than half the width of the portal vein is unlikely to be abnormal.[121]
  • Ask before you call it dilated: age, and any previous cholecystectomy.
  • Context: when the gallbladder and the liver and pancreas blood tests are normal, an isolated dilated duct is very uncommon.[122] A dilated duct can come from a stone, a pancreatic mass, or previous cholecystectomy.
Check yourselfYou see two small anechoic tubes in front of the portal vein and measure the larger at 7 mm. What must you do before reporting a dilated bile duct?

Apply color Doppler. If the structure you measured fills with color, you have measured the hepatic artery.

Then ask about age and cholecystectomy. Seven millimetres is at the upper limit in a younger adult with a gallbladder, but can be normal in an elderly patient or after cholecystectomy.

07The gallbladder

#ApproachTechnique
1Subcostal, via the fissureSubcostal on a deep breath, supine or left lateral. Follow the main lobar fissure from the portal vein: the gallbladder sits at its end.
2IntercostalSupine or left lateral. Find the portal vein, then look in front of the hilum in long axis for the gallbladder neck.
3Liver–kidney viewThe view you use for liver–kidney brightness. The gallbladder lies medial to the kidney, below the liver.
  • Fan through the whole gallbladder in long axis, then rotate and fan through it in short axis. Pay attention to the neck, where a single stone can hide.
  • Measure the anterior wall in short axis, with the probe perpendicular. Normal is 3 mm or less.
  • If you see a stone, roll the patient to the left and watch whether it moves.
  • A gallbladder full of stones gives a bright wall line, a thin dark line, a bright stone line and a shadow: the wall–echo–shadow sign.
  • Pitfalls: after a meal the gallbladder is small and its wall looks thick even when normal. The duodenum can mimic a thick-walled, stone-filled gallbladder.

Source:[122]

08When you cannot see it

ProblemWhat to try, in order
Bowel gasApply pressure, then left lateral decubitus.
Rib shadowsRotate the probe to lie along the rib space. Ask for a deep breath. Raise the right arm.
Top of the liver not seenIntercostal views angled up. Change position.
Patient cannot hold a breathTry shorter sweeps.
Lower bile duct hidden by gasLeft lateral decubitus. If still unable to see despite best efforts then report it as not fully seen.
Check yourselfIn a large patient you see the left lobe and mid right lobe well, but never the top of the liver, despite repositioning. No lesion is seen. How do you report it?

As a limited study. State that the top of the liver was not adequately seen, that the visualized liver showed no focal lesion, and recommend repeat or alternative imaging.

Do not write “no focal lesion, normal study.”

09Match the probe position to the view

Match each probe position to the ultrasound image it produces. Every option is shown at once. Select a probe position, then the image you think it gives (you can also pick the image first). A correct match flashes green and reveals the full image with its explanation. A wrong match flashes red, so try again until it is right.

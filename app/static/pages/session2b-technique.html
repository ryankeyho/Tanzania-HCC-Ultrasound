Week 1 · Session 2b

Scanning Technique: Controls, Movements and the Protocol

A screening ultrasound is only as good as the volume of liver it actually covered. Most missed lesions are not subtle findings that were seen and misread, they are regions that were never imaged.

By the end of this session you should be able to

  • Set up the machine, and familiarize yourself with the controls
  • Use the five transducer movements deliberately and name what each is for
  • Choose a patient position for each part of the study, and coach the breath-hold
  • Work through the full screening protocol in sequence
  • Find, identify, and correctly measure the common bile duct
  • Recognize when a study is incomplete, and say so in the report

01Setting up

The most common reason images look poor at the start of scanning is that the gain, depth, window, or operator position is not optimal. Learn to set up in a fixed sequence before you start scanning.

OrderControlSet it to
1PresetAbdomen. The preset often sets frequency, dynamic range and processing appropriate to depth.
2ProbeCurved array. The linear end has exactly one liver job, covered in Session 3.
3DepthRegion of interest fills most of the screen. Set this before gain.
4Overall gainVessels look properly black, parenchyma mid-gray. If in doubt, slightly under-gained beats over-gained.
Establish, then correct deliberately Establish baseline settings using the sequence above, then make small, deliberate depth/gain corrections when depth or window genuinely changes, rather than repeatedly reaching for gain to fix a problem it will not solve. If the image is poor, reposition the probe and patient before repeatedly changing controls; a different position, window, or breath-hold usually does more than another setting tweak.

02The five movements

Two hand technique, from the start Right hand on the transducer executing the five movements below. Left hand free for depth, gain, freeze, Doppler, and saving images, making small deliberate corrections when depth or window genuinely changes rather than leaving settings untouched for the entire sweep. A sweep from the dome to the inferior edge passes through several centimeters of depth change, and gain occasionally needs a small correction to match. Reaching across with the probe hand to adjust a control, even briefly, breaks the sweep and usually means restarting it.
01Slide, move to new anatomy
Translating the probe across the skin, keeping it perpendicular. This changes where you are, not what angle you are looking from. Use it to move between intercostal spaces, or to follow a structure along its length.
02Fan (tilt), sweep through the volume
Keeping the footprint in one place and angling the beam through the tissue, like opening a fan. This is the movement that covers a volume. A static plane images a slice; fanning images the organ.
03Rock (heel-toe), shift the near field
Tilting along the long axis of the probe, within the scan plane, so one end of the probe presses in more. Use it to bring a structure into the center of the image without losing it.
04Rotate, change the scan plane
Turning the probe about its own axis to move between transverse, longitudinal, and oblique planes. This is how you get the second orthogonal view of a lesion, and how you align the probe parallel within an intercostal space to avoid rib shadow.
05Compress, displace what is in the way
Graded pressure to push bowel gas out of the way. Particularly helpful over the left lobe of the liver. Apply it gradually and watch the patient's face to ensure they are not in discomfort.

Learners consistently under-compress. The instinct is to press lightly, but light pressure will likely not be sufficient to displace bowel gas. Apply firm pressure while checking in with the patient that they are not in pain.

04Liver Scanning

The key is to perform your scans in the same way each time. This ensures that no parts are missed or overlooked. A non-systematic approach increases the likelihood of missed regions and lesions.

The sweep, in order

Transverse first, in three columns. Then longitudinal, as one continuous rocking sweep. Then the targeted views.

  1. Set up. Patient fasted where possible, supine, abdomen exposed from xiphoid to iliac crest. Place gel on the skin. Machine set per the order in §01.
  2. Transverse, left column. Probe as high under the costal margin as it will go. Fan cranially first to catch the top of the liver, fan back through neutral, then step caudally and repeat down the column.
  3. Transverse, middle column. Same pattern, high, fan up, fan back, step down. The hepatic venous confluence appears on the cranial fan here.
  4. Transverse, right column. Same again, sliding laterally until the liver edge leaves the screen.
  5. Longitudinal sweep. Start sagittal on the patient's left. Rock the probe up and down within the plane while sliding steadily across to the right. The rock covers craniocaudal extent; the slide carries you across.
  6. Right lobe, intercostal. Step down the spaces, footprint parallel within each, on quiet breathing or a gentle half-breath. This is the step that covers the dome.
  7. Porta hepatis. Transverse for the Mickey Mouse, then the shoulder–umbilicus oblique for the long axis. Measure the CBD (§05).
  8. Gallbladder. Fan through it in long axis wall to wall, then sweep transverse fundus to neck. Can repeat in a second position if not well visualized to assess stone movement.
  9. Hepatorenal recess. Free fluid, and liver–kidney echogenicity in one frame.
  10. Portal vein Doppler. Patency and flow direction. Read the color bar before the color.

Store and label a representative image at each step. If a step has no stored image, treat that as a flag, not an omission. The full view-by-view list with breathing instructions is in the Required Views reference.

Fan up before you go down In every transverse column, the first movement is cranial. The most common error is starting at a comfortable mid-abdominal position and working downward, everything above the starting point is then never imaged, and that is the dome, where lesions hide.
Measuring liver size honestly The standard measurement is the maximum craniocaudal extent in the midclavicular line (MCL). The number you get depends heavily on body habitus and how deep a breath the patient took; the same liver measured on a slim, asthenic build versus a larger build can read several centimeters apart purely from the angle the probe sits at, without the liver itself being any different size.

Use a single, consistent technique: true craniocaudal span in the MCL, probe angled to capture the full superior-inferior extent in one plane. A commonly used working upper limit for this measurement is approximately 13 cm, though normal ranges vary somewhat by source and body habitus, so treat a borderline reading as a prompt to repeat the measurement carefully rather than a sharp cutoff.
Two planes, always Every region gets covered in two orthogonal planes, and every suspected lesion gets confirmed in both. A finding visible in only one plane is likely artifact. This systematic approach prevents both false positives and false negatives or missed lesions.

05The porta hepatis and the common bile duct

The bile duct is the structure most often overlooked when scanning, as it can be difficult to appreciate. In a screening context it matters, though, because biliary dilatation alongside a hepatic mass shifts the differential toward cholangiocarcinoma rather than HCC, and it points to obstruction that needs its own workup.

Three segments, three named views

Each segment has its own landmark and its own scan, and each scan has a memorable name worth actually using, because a name recalls the technique faster than a description does.

SegmentNamed viewLandmark & technique
Intrahepatic“Hilum eye”Portal bifurcation, intrahepatic, anterior to the vein. Normally not seen at all alongside portal branches, if you see a duct here, it is dilated.
Hilar / periportal“Emergency view”Shoulder–umbilicus oblique section, supine or left lateral, subcostal or intercostal. Portal vein and hepatic artery at the porta, the segment you measure, and the one to fall back on when time is short.
Retropancreatic / distal“Cyst in the pancreatic head”Pancreatic head in transverse section. The duct appears as a small round anechoic “cyst” within the head, frequently obscured by duodenal gas, and where obstructing lesions most often sit.

Finding it

Start at the porta hepatis and get a transverse view, the “Emergency view” above. You are looking for the “Mickey Mouse” arrangement: the portal vein forms the head, with the hepatic artery and the common duct as the two ears sitting anteriorly. At this level, proximal to where the cystic duct joins, the duct seen here is often anatomically the common hepatic duct rather than the common bile duct proper; "common duct" is used deliberately to avoid overstating that distinction on ultrasound alone. Then rotate to the long axis of the portal vein, the duct runs anterior and parallel to it.

If you are not sure what you are looking at, follow it A structure you cannot immediately classify as vessel, duct, or lesion usually resolves itself if you track it in the direction it runs. Vessels and ducts course somewhere, follow them along their length and they lead to a hilum, a confluence, or out of the field of view entirely. A lesion does not: if it does not appear to travel anywhere as you slide or fan along its long axis, it is not a vessel or duct, and that itself raises concern for a focal lesion rather than a tubular structure caught in cross-section.
Color Doppler helps distinguish them The hepatic artery and the duct look identical on B-mode: two small anechoic tubes side by side. Put a color box over them, with scale, gain, and angle optimized. Positive arterial flow identifies the artery. Absence of color is only supportive of the duct once Doppler settings are properly optimized, it does not prove it on its own, since a poorly angled or low-flow vessel can also show no color. Do it every time, not only when unsure.
The distal view is the one worth practicing It reaches the segment that is both hardest to see and most likely to contain the lesion causing the obstruction. Finding the duct in cross-section within the pancreatic head first, then rotating to follow it, works better than trying to chase the duct downward from the hilum through duodenal gas.

Measuring it

  • Measure on the longitudinal view, at the widest point of the extrahepatic duct
  • Inner wall to inner wall, the lumen, not including the walls
  • Perpendicular to the duct's long axis
  • The distal duct is frequently obscured by duodenal gas. If you cannot see it, say so rather than reporting the visible portion as if it were the whole duct

What is normal

Measured on the “Emergency view,” longitudinal, inner wall to inner wall:

SituationUpper limit
Intact gallbladderUnder about 7 mm
After cholecystectomyUnder about 10 mm

Always ask about prior cholecystectomy before calling a duct dilated, the same number means something different depending on the answer.

Two more numbers worth knowing

Duct diameter increases modestly with age, so mild dilatation to 8–9 mm can be physiological in an older patient with no other obstructive features, age belongs in the same mental checklist as prior cholecystectomy. The duct wall itself should be imperceptible; over about 1.5 mm is abnormal in its own right, independent of the lumen diameter.

Check yourselfYou see two small anechoic tubes anterior to the portal vein and measure the larger at 7 mm. What must you do before reporting a dilated bile duct?

Apply color Doppler. If the structure you measured fills with color, you have measured the hepatic artery.

Then ask about cholecystectomy and age. Seven millimetres in a younger, intact-gallbladder adult warrants attention. The same number in someone older, or with a remote cholecystectomy, may be physiological.

07Reasons for Poor Visualization

Difficult studies are the norm in screening, for a few common reasons.

ProblemWhat to do, in order
Bowel gas over the left lobeGraded compression → reposition → oblique subxiphoid approach
Rib shadows fragmenting the imageRotate the probe parallel within the space, then slide; do not tilt into the rib
Dome not visualizedLeft lateral decubitus → steeper cranial angulation from an intercostal window → try quiet expiration
Patient cannot breath-holdShort bursts at end-inspiration; more images, shorter sweeps; note it in the report
Duct obscured by duodenal gasLeft lateral decubitus; report the duct as incompletely visualized if it stays unvisualized

If all attempts fail, the study is limited. Report which region was not seen and what limited it. A limited study honestly labeled is far more useful than a "normal" study that quietly excluded a portion.

Check yourselfYou complete a study in a large patient. Left lobe and mid right lobe well seen; the dome was never adequately imaged despite repositioning. No lesion seen. How do you report it?

Limited study. State explicitly that the hepatic dome was not adequately visualized, that the visualized liver showed no focal lesion, and recommend repeat imaging or an alternative modality.

What you must not write is "no focal lesion, normal study." The dome is precisely where an unseen lesion is most likely to be, so a negative report here carries a specific, known blind spot, and the person reading your report has no way to know that unless you say so.
